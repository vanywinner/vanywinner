A Quarantined Relative Becomes Kenya’s Second Ebola Patient

News

By Newsroom, 14 Oct, 18:26

Kenya’s health authorities got the warning they were hoping not to need on Wednesday. A man held in mandatory isolation after a relative’s death from Ebola has himself tested positive, bringing the country’s confirmed total to two cases and one death.

The chain of events began with a Kenyan who made his way home from the Democratic Republic of Congo by way of Uganda. He had taken ill in Congo roughly a month earlier, and by the time he reached a Nairobi hospital the disease was far advanced. He died there on October 5.

His family was placed under a 21-day quarantine. On October 13, one member of that household, a 39-year-old man, developed fever, exhaustion and aching joints. Two laboratories, the National Virology Reference Laboratory and the KEMRI-CDC facility, ran their own tests and reached the same result. The pathogen is Bundibugyo, an uncommon species of the Ebola virus.

Health Director General Patrick Amoth announced the findings, and officials were keen to frame them as evidence that the system worked. Because the patient was already being watched, there was no gap between falling ill and being identified, and care could begin at once. The ministry says nothing points to the virus spreading through the wider community. A suspected infection in Wajir, which had briefly drawn attention, came back negative.

How many people the first patient exposed remains unclear. Published figures vary widely: 128 contacts in one account, 57 identified and 10 quarantined in earlier reporting, and 66 in quarantine in a headline. Tracing is an ongoing process, so the totals have likely climbed since the earliest counts. The ministry’s next briefing is the safer source.

The case also places Kenya on a growing list. It is the fourth country to report an infection tied to the Congolese epidemic, now the second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded, with more than 8,000 cases and upwards of 4,000 deaths. That a sick traveller could cross multiple borders and reach the capital has sharpened scrutiny of screening along the region’s frontiers.

Advice on what to do next pulls in different directions. The World Health Organization wants targeted screening rather than sweeping travel bans, while Amnesty International has asked that the response not trample on rights or turn into excessive surveillance.

The coming days will test whether the quarantine that exposed this case can also contain it. That depends on whether contact tracing keeps pace, and on whether the government can protect public health without eroding civil liberties.
