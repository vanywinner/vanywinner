Why Paul Mackenzie May Serve Only 133 Years Despite 200-Year Jail Term

News

By Vanywinner KE, 7 Nov, 1:45

Kenya’s prison laws allow inmates to earn a one-third sentence remission for good conduct, potentially reducing the Shakahola cult leader’s lengthy prison term.

Nairobi: Shakahola cult leader Paul Mackenzie could serve approximately 133 years in prison despite receiving a 200-year sentence for the murder of 191 people, thanks to provisions in Kenyan law that allow prisoners to earn time off their sentences.

Mackenzie was sentenced by Justice Diana Kavedza at the Mombasa High Court on Friday, November 6, 2026, after being convicted on 191 counts of murder linked to the Shakahola tragedy.

The court handed him 100 years in prison for 183 counts of murder by starvation and another 100 years for eight counts of murder by direct homicide. The two sentences were ordered to run consecutively, bringing his total jail term to 200 years.

However, the sentence could be reduced under Section 46 of the Prisons Act, which allows eligible prisoners to earn a remission of up to one-third of their sentence for good conduct and industry while in prison.

If the full one-third remission is granted, Mackenzie’s 200-year sentence would effectively fall to approximately 133 years and four months.

The reduction, however, is not automatic. The law places the authority to grant remission in the hands of the Commissioner of Prisons, and a prisoner’s conduct plays a key role in determining whether the benefit is awarded or withdrawn.

Prisoners who violate prison regulations may lose their remission, meaning Mackenzie could still be required to serve a longer period depending on how prison authorities assess his conduct.

The application of remission to prisoners convicted of serious crimes has also been shaped by court decisions. In 2019, the High Court in Machakos declared unconstitutional provisions that excluded certain prisoners from earning remission, opening the door for capital offenders to benefit from the reduction.

Despite the possibility of a shorter sentence, Mackenzie is unlikely to serve either 200 years or 133 years in full, given the limits of human life. His eventual release, if any, would also depend on the outcome of appeals and the application of prison regulations.

For now, the 200-year sentence stands, with the possibility of remission offering a legal route to a reduced term rather than a guaranteed early release.
